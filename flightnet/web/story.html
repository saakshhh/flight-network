1 · Hubs

A handful of airports carry the network

Most airports are small: the median one has nonstop flights to just [[hubs.median_degree]] destinations, and [[hubs.small_share]]% have two or fewer. A few are enormous. [[hubs.top.0.city]] reaches [[hubs.top.0.degree]] airports nonstop, [[hubs.top.1.city]] [[hubs.top.1.degree]], [[hubs.top.2.city]] [[hubs.top.2.degree]].

The top 5% of airports ([[hubs.top5_count]] of them) are an endpoint of [[hubs.top5_pair_share]]% of all routes. This is what network scientists call a heavy-tailed network: no "typical" airport, just many spokes and a few hubs.

2 · Small world

[[reach.within.4]]% of airport pairs are four flights or fewer apart

Hubs make the world small. Over every pair of the [[overview.main_airports]] connected airports, the fastest connection takes [[reach.mean_flights]] flights on average; [[reach.within.3]]% of pairs need three or fewer and [[reach.within.6]]% need six or fewer. From Chicago O'Hare, [[reach.from_ord.1]] airports are one flight away, [[reach.from_ord.2]] are two, and [[reach.from_ord.3]] are within three.

Pick an airport to see how many flights everywhere else takes. Hover an airport for the itinerary.

The two airports farthest apart are [[reach.far_a.name]] in Washington State and [[reach.far_b.name]] in northern Quebec: [[reach.diameter]] flights ([[reach.far_route]]), because both sit at the end of chains of tiny regional airports and the only big airports on the way are [[reach.far_via]].

3 · Gateways

The most important airport isn't the busiest

Counting destinations is one way to rank airports. Betweenness is another: the share of all fastest connections in the network that pass through an airport. It measures how much an airport holds the network together.

By betweenness, the world's #1 airport is Anchorage, with just [[gateways.anc_degree]] destinations (#[[gateways.anc_degree_rank]] by size). Its score is [[gateways.anc_vs_ams]]× Amsterdam's, even though Amsterdam has seven times the destinations. Anchorage is the only link between Alaska's bush airports and the rest of the world, so every trip from one of them to anywhere else goes through it. Kangerlussuaq in Greenland, with [[gateways.sfj_degree]] destinations, ranks #[[gateways.sfj_rank]] for the same reason. [[gateways.top20_outside_top50_degree]] of the top 20 airports by betweenness aren't in the top 50 by destinations.

4 · Fragility

Losing the gateways hurts more than losing the biggest hubs

What if airports closed? Close [[fragility.k]] random airports and about [[fragility.cut_random]] others lose their only links. Close the [[fragility.k]] with the most destinations and [[fragility.cut_deg]] are cut off. Close the [[fragility.k]] with the highest betweenness and [[fragility.cut_btw]] airports ([[fragility.cut_btw_share]]% of the network) are stranded, [[fragility.cut_btw_alaska]] of them in Alaska.

The big hubs are heavily backed up: Frankfurt's passengers can reroute via Munich or Amsterdam. Gateways have no backup. After 100 closures in betweenness order, only [[fragility.remaining_100.betweenness]]% of airports are still connected, against [[fragility.remaining_100.degree]]% when the busiest close first and [[fragility.remaining_100.random]]% at random.

5 · Airlines

Two business models, visible from space

Look at one airline at a time and two strategies appear. Hub-and-spoke carriers route almost everything through home: [[airlines.QR.share]]% of [[airlines.QR.name]] routes start or end in Doha, [[airlines.EK.share]]% of [[airlines.EK.name]] routes in Dubai, [[airlines.TK.share]]% of [[airlines.TK.name]] routes in Istanbul. Point-to-point carriers criss-cross between many bases: only [[airlines.FR.share]]% of the [[airlines.FR.routes]] [[airlines.FR.name]] routes touch its busiest airport, London Stansted, and [[airlines.WN.share]]% of [[airlines.WN.name]] routes touch Chicago Midway.

Of the [[airlines.n]] airlines operating at least [[airlines.min_pairs]] routes, [[airlines.over_half]] send more than half of their routes through one airport and [[airlines.under_quarter]] fewer than a quarter. US network carriers sit in between ([[airlines.DL.name]]: [[airlines.DL.share]]% at Atlanta) because they run several hubs.

6 · Regions

The network draws its own map

Community detection groups airports that mostly connect to each other, without being told anything about geography. It finds [[regions.n]] groups, [[regions.big]] of them with 100+ airports, and they mostly match continents. The exceptions are telling: India, Iran and the Arabian Peninsula form one region around the Gulf hubs; Russia and Central Asia form their own; Alaska, Northern Canada and Greenland each form a separate world, tied to everything else through one or two gateways. That is the same structure that makes those gateways so important in chapter 3.

What it adds up to

Hubs shrink the world

A few hundred hub airports put [[reach.within.4]]% of all airport pairs within four flights of each other.

Size isn't importance

Small gateways like Anchorage hold remote regions on the network. Rankings by traffic miss them; betweenness finds them.

Redundancy is uneven

Big hubs back each other up. Gateways don't, so they are where the network is fragile.

Data and methods

Data

  • OpenFlights route database (ODbL): [[overview.routes]] airline routes, merged into [[overview.pairs]] airport pairs. It is a 2014 snapshot and has no flight frequencies or seat counts, so a route counts once whether it runs hourly or weekly.
  • Airport locations from OpenFlights, with continents and the airports OpenFlights lacks filled in from OurAirports (public domain). Country outlines: Natural Earth.
  • OpenFlights reuses two-letter airline codes (VY is both Vueling and Formosa Airlines), so the build names each code by the home country its routes actually serve, and leaves unclear ones unnamed rather than guessing.

Network measures

  • Airports are nodes; an edge joins two airports when any airline flies between them nonstop.
  • Destinations = degree. Flights needed = shortest-path length, over all pairs in the connected network ([[overview.main_share]]% of airports; the rest are isolated island networks).
  • Betweenness = share of all shortest paths through an airport (NetworkX, exact).
  • Fragility: remove airports in a fixed order and measure the largest connected group left; random order averaged over 20 runs.
  • Regions: Louvain community detection. Hub share: share of an airline's own (non-codeshare) routes that touch its busiest airport.

Every number on this page is computed from the data by flightnet/stats.py when the site is built.
